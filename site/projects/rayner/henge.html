Why does sunset line up with Manhattan’s streets?

Look west down a straight street. Buildings frame a narrow direction, and the low Sun fits inside it. The buildings do not move tomorrow. To explain the alignment, we need to follow the Sun’s changing path.

Transcript

Street direction: Manhattan: 29° north of west

  1. The street stays still. Why does this evening matter?

    Predict: will the low Sun line up again tomorrow?

    Look west down a straight street. Buildings frame a narrow direction, and the low Sun fits inside it. The buildings do not move tomorrow. To explain the alignment, we need to follow the Sun’s changing path.

    • 42nd Street: about 29° north of west.
  2. One axis direction, opposite sides of the Sun

    Earth turns each day and travels around the Sun each year.

    Earth’s axis keeps nearly the same direction through the year. In June the north leans sunward; in December it leans away. The red observer and the small horizon line turn with Earth. We now look from that observer’s position to see where the daily Sun path meets the horizon. Sizes and distances here are schematic.

    • Sun. N. June. North sunward. N. December. North away. New York: red dot and horizon.
  3. The daily paths meet the horizon in different places

    Predict: is June’s sunset north or south of due west?

    The street stays fixed while Earth’s tilt moves the sunset direction across it and back. These are afternoon paths seen from New York, ending where the Sun’s centre reaches a flat horizon. In June more of the daily path lies above that horizon, and sunset is north of west. December’s shorter path ends south of west. Daily turning carries the Sun along a path; the changing tilt toward the Sun shifts which path we see.

    • New York’s horizon. south. west. northward. December. Equinox. June.
  4. Follow the sunset direction through a year

    Predict: how often does the yellow curve cross the red street?

    The yellow curve marks sunset direction, from March through the following March. Higher means farther north along the horizon. The red line stays at this street’s bearing. It lies near the northern turning point in June. The calendar spacing is an idealised circular orbit, not a viewing timetable.

    • Street: 29° N of west. Yellow: sunset direction. Mar. Jun. Sep. Dec. Mar.
  5. Across the street in May, back across it in July

    Change the street setting: the sky stays the same.

    The sunset direction passes the street before June, turns a little farther north, then returns across it. This gives the May and July alignment seasons. Changing the street changes the direction we wait for, not Earth’s motion.

    • Street: 29° N of west. Yellow: sunset direction. Mar. Jun. Sep. Dec. Mar. May. July.
  6. One bearing still leaves the Sun’s height to explain

    Half Sun and full Sun compare neighbouring evenings.

    The two views keep the Sun centred down the same street. Half Sun puts its centre on the visible horizon; full Sun puts its bottom edge there. During the May northward sweep the aligned Sun rises from half to full on neighbouring evenings; during the July return it reverses. These are changes across dates, not stages of one evening’s descent. The disc is enlarged here.

    • Half Sun. Full Sun. Centre on horizon. Bottom on horizon. May: half → full. July: full → half.

Check yourself

  1. The Sun is centred down the street, but its centre is above the horizon. Is that enough to identify a half-Sun event?

    Show the answer

    No. The horizontal alignment fixes its bearing, not its height. Half Sun puts the centre on the visible horizon; full Sun puts the bottom edge there.

  2. Suppose another street points farther north than the northernmost sunset in this model. How many low-Sun alignments does it get?

    Show the answer

    None. The annual curve must cross the street’s direction. Moving the street beyond its northern turning point removes both crossings.
