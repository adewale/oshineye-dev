Why does sunset line up with Manhattan’s streets?

Stand on the Tudor City overpass and look west down 42nd Street. At 8:14 pm on 25 May 2026 the setting Sun sat half above the horizon in the slot between the buildings, drawn true size. The Hayden Planetarium calls this Manhattanhenge. The street points the same way all year, so what changes must be where the Sun sets.

  3. From above: sunset lines up with 42nd Street, sunrise does not

    North is up; the grid is schematic. Gold arrows: the Sun at sunset and sunrise on an alignment day.

    From above, 42nd Street points 29.0° north of west, measured between two OpenStreetMap points 2.7 km apart, and the same angle south of east at the other end. On an alignment evening the setting Sun lies on that line. That morning it rose 29.0° north of east, 58.1° from the street’s other end, which faces the sunrise only on winter mornings around the December solstice.

    • Sunset. 29.0° north of west. Sunrise. 29.0° north of east. N. East end. 29.0° south of east. Sunrise misses that end by 58.1°.
  4. The Sun sets toward the place where it stands overhead

    Earth from space: the place, its street carried on, and where the Sun is overhead.

    At any moment the Sun stands overhead at one point on Earth, and from New York it sets toward that point once the point is a quarter of the way round the globe away. At 8:14 pm on 25 May it was overhead at 21.1° N, over the Pacific, where 42nd Street, carried on along the ground, meets that ring. Through the year the point slides between the tropics, crossing the street’s line twice.

    • Earth from space, with New York on it. A red line carries the street on along the ground to a gold ring a quarter of the way round the globe, meeting it at 21.2° north. The Sun stands overhead on that ring at half Sun: on 25 May at 21.1° north, on 18 March at 0.7° south.
  5. June’s Sun sets 31.7° north of west, December’s 31.7° south

    Red: the street’s arc, rising from the horizon where the street points, 29.0° north of west.

June’s circle lies more above the horizon and ends 31.7° north of west, 2.6° beyond the street. December’s shorter path ends 31.7° south of west, far short of it. The circle shifts because Earth’s axis is tilted.

Check yourself

  1. Another city’s streets point 10° north of west, between Manhattan’s 29° and due west. Are its two alignment seasons nearer the June solstice than Manhattan’s, or farther from it?

    Show the answer

    Farther from it. The sunset direction sweeps north from December to June and back; it passes 10° north of west before it reaches 29° on the way north, and after it on the way back, so the two seasons sit nearer the equinoxes.

  2. Suppose another street points farther north than the northernmost sunset at its latitude. How many low-Sun alignments does it get?

    Show the answer

    None. The annual curve must cross the street’s direction. Moving the street beyond its northern turning point removes both crossings.

Sources

Checked against a recording made once, which the model’s tests compare with: Skyfield 1.55 with JPL’s DE440s, and ERFA’s horizon coordinates (pyerfa 2.0.1.5).

How it was checked

Skyfield 1.55 observing the Sun through JPL’s DE440s ephemeris, recorded by oracle/sun.py into fixtures/henge-sun.json: the model’s solar position at 74 instants, day and night, to 0.015°; its half- and full-Sun moments on every evening of 2026 at both places to 5 seconds and 0.01°; and its alignment evenings, exactly. The places are OpenStreetMap nodes and each street’s bearing GeographicLib 2.1’s WGS84 geodesic between two of them, recorded in the same fixture. The Hayden Planetarium’s published 2026 minutes are a cross-check: at each the ephemeris puts the Sun 0.12°–0.20° north of 42nd Street, and its dates fall two to four days nearer the solstice than a flat horizon gives. The globe's coastlines are Natural Earth 1:110m (v5.1.2), written to land.ts by oracle/land.py. The idealised sky is checked against 48 vectors from ERFA’s hd2ae (pyerfa 2.0.1.5, oracle/henge.py, fixtures/henge-erfa.json) and USNO’s altitude formula. All in model.test.ts; scene.test.ts checks the pictures against the model.
