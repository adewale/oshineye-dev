Why does sunset line up with Manhattan’s streets?

Look west down 42nd Street. On a few evenings a year, in late May and mid-July, the low Sun fits between the buildings; the Hayden Planetarium calls it Manhattanhenge. The buildings point the same way all year, so what changes must be where the Sun sets.

Transcript

Street direction: Manhattan: 29° north of west

  1. Why does the Sun line up on so few evenings?

    Predict: will the low Sun line up again tomorrow?

    Look west down 42nd Street. On a few evenings a year, in late May and mid-July, the low Sun fits between the buildings; the Hayden Planetarium calls it Manhattanhenge. The buildings point the same way all year, so what changes must be where the Sun sets.

    • 42nd Street: about 29° north of west.
  2. From above: sunset lines up with 42nd Street, sunrise does not

    North is up; the grid is schematic. Gold arrows: the Sun at sunset and sunrise on an alignment day.

    The street stays fixed while Earth’s tilt moves the sunset direction across it and back. From above, Manhattan’s cross streets point 29.1° north of west, and the same angle south of east at the other end. On an alignment evening the setting Sun lies on that line and shines straight down 42nd Street. That morning it rose 29.1° north of east, 58.2° from the street’s other end, which faces the sunrise only on winter mornings either side of the December solstice.

    • Sunset. 29.1° north of west. Sunrise. 29.1° north of east. N. East end. 29.1° south of east. Sunrise misses that end by 58.2°.
  3. June’s Sun sets 31.7° north of west, December’s 31.7° south

    Red: the street’s arc, rising from the horizon where the street points, 29.1° north of west.

    New York’s sky as a dome, seen from outside, west of it. Each day the Sun’s centre goes round a circle about the celestial pole; the bold arc is its day, ending at a flat horizon. In June more of the circle lies above the horizon and it ends 31.7° north of west, 2.6° beyond the street. December’s shorter path ends 31.7° south of west, far short of it. The circle shifts because Earth’s axis is tilted: the north leans toward the Sun in June, away in December.

    • N. S. W. December. Equinox. June. Street. Pole. New York’s horizon.
  4. Follow the sunset through a year

    Predict: how many times will the sunset cross the street?

    Now let the year run, from the December solstice to the next. The Sun’s daily circle slides north, and its sunset, the gold band along the horizon, travels north with it. It passes the street, keeps going to its June turning point, then comes back south and passes the street again. One fixed direction, crossed on the way out and on the way back. Each day’s circle comes from the Sun’s declination in an idealised circular orbit.

    • The dome with one day’s path. Its Sun sets 31.7° south of west. Since the December solstice the sunset has crossed the street twice.
  5. Across the street in May, back across it in July

    Change the street setting: the sky stays the same.

    The same year as a chart: across, March to the following March; up, how far north along the horizon the Sun sets. The line labelled Street stays at this street’s bearing. The sunset direction passes the street before June, turns a little farther north, then returns across it. This gives the May and July alignment seasons. Changing the street changes the direction we wait for, not Earth’s motion. The calendar spacing is an idealised circular orbit, not a viewing timetable.

    • Street: 29° N of west. Yellow: sunset direction. due west. Mar. Jun. Sep. Dec. Mar. May. July.
  6. One axis direction, opposite sides of the Sun

    Why the Sun’s daily circle shifts: Earth’s tilt, seen from beside its orbit.

    Earth’s axis keeps nearly the same direction through the year. In June the north leans sunward; in December it leans away. The red dot is New York at noon, on the circle Earth’s daily turn carries it round: gold where that circle is in sunlight, dark where it is in night. In June 62% of the circle is in sunlight, in December 38%. Sizes and distances here are schematic.

    • Sun. N. N. June. December. North sunward. North away. Red dot: New York at noon, on its daily circle. Gold: the circle in daylight; dark: in night.
  7. From the sky to the street: bearing and height in one view

    Stand at the dome’s centre and lower your eyes along the street.

    The calendar names four evenings: half Sun and full Sun in each season. Stand in the middle of the street, at the dome’s centre, and lower your eyes along the street’s circle, labelled Street, to the horizon. From the centre, the street’s circle, the horizon and the buildings’ edges stay straight, as a camera sees them. Left and right say whether the Sun is centred between the buildings; up and down say how much of its disc is above the horizon.

    • Looking along the street from its middle, 3° above the horizon: the street’s circle runs straight up the middle, and the Sun’s path on a May evening comes down to the horizon where the street points, with the Sun’s centre on the horizon.
  8. One bearing still leaves the Sun’s height to explain

    Half Sun and full Sun come 1.2 days apart in this model.

    Both views keep the Sun centred down the street. Half Sun puts its centre on the visible horizon; full Sun puts its bottom edge there. During the May sweep the aligned Sun rises from half to full: full Sun comes 1.2 days after half Sun, about one evening to the next. During the July return the order reverses. These are neighbouring dates, not stages of one evening’s descent.

    • Half Sun. Full Sun. Centre on horizon. Bottom on horizon. May: half → full. July: full → half.

Check yourself

  1. Another city’s streets point 10° north of west, between Manhattan’s 29° and due west. Are its two alignment seasons nearer the June solstice than Manhattan’s, or farther from it?

    Show the answer

    Farther from it. The sunset direction sweeps north from December to June and back; it passes 10° north of west before it reaches 29° on the way north, and after it on the way back, so the two seasons sit nearer the equinoxes.

  2. Suppose another street points farther north than the northernmost sunset in this model. How many low-Sun alignments does it get?

    Show the answer

    None. The annual curve must cross the street’s direction. Moving the street beyond its northern turning point removes both crossings.
