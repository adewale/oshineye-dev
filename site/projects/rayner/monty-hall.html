Why does the host’s rule change the odds of switching?

A car is behind one of three doors, and goats are behind the other two. You pick A. The host knows where the car is; he opens B, shows a goat, and offers you C. With two doors still closed, the odds look even.

Transcript

Host rule: Knows where the car is; opens a goat at random

  1. You pick A; the host opens B: a goat

    Should you switch? That depends on what the host knew and how he chose which door to open.

    A car is behind one of three doors, and goats are behind the other two. You pick A. The host knows where the car is; he opens B, shows a goat, and offers you C. With two doors still closed, the odds look even.

    Try it: send stay, switch.

    • Doors A, B and C; A is your pick; B is open and shows a goat; C is closed.
  2. Stay with A, or switch to C?

    Predict: does it matter what the host knew and how he picked a door?

    Two doors are closed and you have seen a goat. Would switching win more often than staying? And would your answer change if the host had opened a door at random and happened to show a goat?

    • Doors A, B and C; A is your pick; B is open and shows a goat; C is closed.
  3. Which of these rounds could open B to a goat?

    Each cell is one way the round can go, drawn as wide as its chance.

    The strip lays out every round for a pick of A, each as wide as its chance. The car is behind each door a third of the time. When it is behind A, both other doors hide goats and he tosses a coin between B and C, a sixth each. When it is behind C, B is the only goat you didn’t pick, so he opens it in that whole third; when it is behind B, he has to open C. You saw B open to a goat. Find the cells that match and add up their widths before going on.

    • Host who knows: car behind A, he opens B: goat, staying wins, 1 sixth; car behind A, he opens C: goat, staying wins, 1 sixth; car behind B, he opens C: goat, switching wins, 2 sixths; car behind C, he opens B: goat, switching wins, 2 sixths. Each cell: opens · shows · wins.
  4. When the host who knows opens B, switching wins 2 in 3

    With the car behind C, he must open B; with it behind A, he opens B half the time.

    Two cells open B to a goat, so the rest are greyed. In one, worth a sixth, the car is behind A and his coin happened to pick B. In the other, worth two sixths, the car is behind C and B was the one goat he could open. Of the 3 sixths left, switching wins 2: 66.7%. Two doors are still closed, and they are not equally likely.

    • Host who knows: car behind A, he opens B: goat, staying wins, 1 sixth; car behind A, he opens C: goat, staying wins, 1 sixth (greyed); car behind B, he opens C: goat, switching wins, 2 sixths (greyed); car behind C, he opens B: goat, switching wins, 2 sixths. Each cell: opens · shows · wins.
  5. One goat behind B, two hosts: switching wins 2 in 3 or 1 in 2

    With the car behind C, the host who knows always opens B; the random host opens it half the time.

    Switching wins 2 in 3 if the host knows where the car is and picks at random between two goats, but 1 in 2 if he opened a random door and showed a goat. With the car behind A, either host opens B in one sixth, and staying wins it. With the car behind C, the host who knows must open B, which gives switching two sixths; the random host opens B in only one of them and opens the car in the other. In both strips, the rounds left are those that match what you saw: B opened to a goat. The goat is the same evidence in both, but the host who knows produces it twice as often when the car is behind C.

    • Host who knows: car behind A, he opens B: goat, staying wins, 1 sixth; car behind A, he opens C: goat, staying wins, 1 sixth (greyed); car behind B, he opens C: goat, switching wins, 2 sixths (greyed); car behind C, he opens B: goat, switching wins, 2 sixths. Random host: car behind A, he opens B: goat, staying wins, 1 sixth; car behind A, he opens C: goat, staying wins, 1 sixth (greyed); car behind B, he opens B: car, game over, 1 sixth (greyed); car behind B, he opens C: goat, switching wins, 1 sixth (greyed); car behind C, he opens B: goat, switching wins, 1 sixth; car behind C, he opens C: car, game over, 1 sixth (greyed). Each cell: opens · shows · wins.

Check yourself

  1. This host knows where the car is. When your first pick is right, he opens one of the two goats at random. When it is wrong, he tosses a coin: heads, he opens the car and the round ends; tails, he opens the other goat. You pick A and he opens B to a goat. How often does switching win, and which strip in the last step is his?

    Show the answer

    50.0%. His strip is the random host’s, cell for cell: with the car behind B or C, he opens each unchosen door half the time, so one of the two sixths in each wrong-pick third shows the car. He knows where the car is, but his rule gives each round the random host’s chance, and those chances set the odds.

  2. The producers hide the car behind A 40% of the time and behind B or C 30% each. You pick A and the host opens B to a goat. How often does switching win with the host who knows, and with the random host?

    Show the answer

    With the host who knows, 60.0%: the car-behind-A rounds now fill 40% of the strip and he opens B in half of them, 20%, while all of the car-behind-C rounds, 30%, open B. With the random host, 42.9%: he opens B in only half of the car-behind-C rounds, 15%, against the same 20%.
