Follow an element: Sodium

  1. Why are the jumps down sodium’s column uneven?

    Predict: what makes the jumps down the column 2, 8, 18 and 32?

    Sodium is element 11, in group 1. Follow the atomic numbers down its column: the gaps change from row to row. The picture shows the numbers and their differences. To explain those jumps, build the rows from the order in which electrons fill an atom, then stack matching runs.

A useful filling rule arranges them in runs, and every full run is 2, 6, 10 or 14 long. Sodium’s 11 are runs of 2, 2 and 6, then 1 into a run of 2.     Runs of 2 at the left, 10 in the middle, 6 at the right, 14 in two rows below.

    Slide every run of 6 to the right edge and every run of 10 into the middle, and lift the runs of 14 into two rows below so the table stays 18 columns wide.   4. The runs have names; helium needs one move

    s holds 2, p holds 6, d holds 10 and f holds 14 electrons.

    Each run is a subshell: s for 2, p for 6, d for 10 and f for 14. Those widths give the table its blocks and its gaps; the changing row lengths explain the jumps down the opening column. Widths alone leave helium over beryllium. The table groups helium with neon: helium’s two electrons complete its first shell, while neon has a complete outer shell of eight. That grouping is information we bring to the table, not something sliding cells proves. The detailed filling rule and its exceptions are in the optional notes.

Under the columns, the s block spans 2, d 10 and p 6; the 14 f columns sit below with no group number.
  5. Reference: filling order and configuration exceptions

    A subshell holds at most 2 electrons (s), 6 (p), 10 (d) or 14 (f).

    Chemists call each run a subshell, named by its shell n and a letter for its length (s 2, p 6, d 10, f 14): sodium’s runs read 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹. A subshell holds at most that many electrons. Subshells fill lowest n + l first, with l from 0 for s to 3 for f, and lower n first on a tie: Madelung’s empirical rule, which NIST’s reference configurations follow for 88 of the first 108 elements. Sodium’s 11 electrons are the solid cells. Helium sits over neon, since 1s² fills shell 1.

Note: Sodium: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹ 3s¹ starts a run of 2: s column 1, group 1. Under the columns, the s block spans 2, d 10 and p 6; the 14 f columns sit below with no group number.
  6. Every group 1 element ends in one s electron

    Each jump is a row: 2 = 2, 8 = 2 + 6, 18 = 2 + 10 + 6, 32 = 2 + 14 + 10 + 6.

    Each badge counts the electrons after the noble gas that closes the row above (every electron, in row 1), leaving out f electrons. Lithium to francium each carry the previous noble gas plus one s electron, and hydrogen’s 1s¹ ends the same way. Each jump is the row the upper member starts: 2 from hydrogen to lithium, 8 from lithium to sodium.

Check yourself

  1. Keep the runs of 14 inside the main table instead of putting them below. How wide would its longest rows be, and why?

    Show the answer

    32 cells: 2 + 14 + 10 + 6. Putting the 14-cell runs below removes 14 columns from those rows, leaving an 18-column main table. The detached rows save width; they are still part of the same filling sequence.

  2. A short row has a run of 2 followed by a run of 6. When its run of 6 is aligned with the rightmost six columns of the 18-column table, which columns are left empty?

    Show the answer

    Columns 3 through 12: ten empty columns. The first two cells stay at the left and the six-cell run starts at column 13. There is no ten-cell run in that row to fill the space between them.
