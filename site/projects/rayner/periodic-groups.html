Why does the periodic table have this shape?

Sodium is element 11, in group 1. Down its column the atomic number rises by 2, 8, 8, 18, 18 and 32. Rows of equal length would give equal jumps, so the lower rows must hold more elements. To see why, add electrons one at a time in the order an atom fills them, then line up the runs they make.

Follow an element: Sodium

  1. Why are the jumps down sodium’s column uneven?

    Predict: what makes the jumps down the column 2, 8, 18 and 32?

    Sodium is element 11, in group 1. Down its column the atomic number rises by 2, 8, 8, 18, 18 and 32. Rows of equal length would give equal jumps, so the lower rows must hold more elements. To see why, add electrons one at a time in the order an atom fills them, then line up the runs they make.

  2. One electron per cell, in runs laid side by side

    Predict: slide the runs so that runs of one length share columns. What shape do you get?

    Give each element a cell and add its electrons one at a time; in a neutral atom the atomic number counts them. A filling rule arranges them in runs, and every full run is 2, 6, 10 or 14 long. Sodium’s 11 are runs of 2, 2 and 6, then 1 into a run of 2. Lay each run beside the last, starting a new row at each run of 2: the rows hold 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32 and 32 cells, and every jump down step 1’s column is one of those row lengths.

  3. Stack runs of one length in the same columns

    Runs of 2 at the left, 10 in the middle, 6 at the right, 14 in two rows below.

    Slide every run of 6 to the right edge and every run of 10 into the middle, and lift the runs of 14 into two rows below so the table stays 18 columns wide. Rows 2 and 3 have no run of 10, so their runs of 6 slide 10 columns and leave a gap. The table’s gaps and widths come from stacking electron-filling runs of 2, 10 and 6, with runs of 14 below. Sodium’s 11th electron starts a run of 2, so it lands in column 1.

  4. The runs have names; helium needs one move

    s holds 2, p holds 6, d holds 10 and f holds 14 electrons.

    Each run is a subshell, named by its shell number and a letter for its length: s holds 2, p 6, d 10 and f 14. By the filling rule, sodium’s runs read 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹. Those widths give the table its blocks and gaps. Widths alone leave helium over beryllium; the table puts it over neon because its 1s² completes shell 1, as 2s² 2p⁶ completes neon’s shell 2. That grouping is chemistry we bring; sliding cells does not prove it.

The run widths are named s, p, d and f, and helium has moved above neon because its first shell is full. Under the columns, the s block spans 2, d 10 and p 6; the 14 f columns sit below with no group number.
  5. Which run fills next: sodium’s 11 electrons in order

    The solid cells are sodium’s 11 electrons, in the order they fill.

    Subshells fill lowest n + l first, where n is the shell and l runs from 0 for s to 3 for f, and lower n first on a tie: Madelung’s empirical rule, which NIST’s reference configurations follow for 88 of the first 108 elements. Sodium’s 3s¹ follows 2p⁶ because n + l is 3 for both and 2p has the lower n.

Note: Sodium: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹ 3s¹ starts a run of 2: s column 1, group 1. Under the columns, the s block spans 2, d 10 and p 6; the 14 f columns sit below with no group number.
  6. Every group 1 element ends in one s electron

    Each jump is a row: 2 = 2, 8 = 2 + 6, 18 = 2 + 10 + 6, 32 = 2 + 14 + 10 + 6.

    Each badge counts the electrons after the noble gas that closes the row above (every electron, in row 1), leaving out f electrons. Lithium to francium each carry the previous noble gas plus one s electron, and hydrogen’s 1s¹ ends the same way. Each jump is the row the upper member starts: 2 from hydrogen to lithium, 8 from lithium to sodium.

Check yourself

  1. Keep the runs of 14 inside the main table instead of putting them below. How wide would its longest rows be, and why?

    Show the answer

    32 cells: 2 + 14 + 10 + 6. Putting the 14-cell runs below removes 14 columns from those rows, leaving an 18-column main table. The detached rows save width; they are still part of the same filling sequence.

  2. Silicon (14) ends 3s² 3p² and tin (50) ends 5s² 4d¹⁰ 5p². Silicon has 4 electrons after neon, tin has 14 after krypton, yet both sit in column 14. Use the stacked runs to explain why.

    Show the answer

    Each lands on the 2nd cell of a run of 6, and runs of 6 start after 2 + 10 columns, so both land in column 2 + 10 + 2 = 14. Tin’s row has a run of 10 (4d¹⁰) that fills columns 3 to 12. Silicon’s row has none, so its run of 6 slides across ten empty columns to reach the same place.
