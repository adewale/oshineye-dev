    It doubles with disk size from 16 MiB to 1 GiB; here 0.0049% of the disk.

    mke2fs sizes the journal in steps of disk size, doubling from 16 MiB at 128 MiB to 1 GiB at 128 GiB, and no larger after that. 20 TiB gets 262,144 blocks, 1 GiB: 1.2% of the metadata and 0.0049% of the disk.

    • Pie chart “The 21,625,887 metadata blocks”: 3 slices:
      • Journal: 1.2% (this step);
      • Inode tables: 97.0%;
      • Other metadata: 1.8%.
    • “Journal by disk size”, 7 labelled values:
      • From 128 GiB: 1 GiB (20 TiB);
      • From 128 MiB: 16 MiB;
      • From 1 GiB: 32 MiB;
      • From 2 GiB: 64 MiB;
      • From 16 GiB: 128 MiB;
      • From 32 GiB: 256 MiB;
      • From 64 GiB: 512 MiB.
  5. The other 392,223 blocks: copies, bitmaps and the first files

    Superblock copies in 25 of 163,840 groups; two bitmaps in every group.

    Group 0, group 1 and groups that are powers of 3, 5 or 7 keep a copy of the superblock and group descriptors: 25 of 163,840 groups. Every group has two bitmaps. The root directory, lost+found, the orphan file and an index block take 518 blocks.

    • Pie chart “The 21,625,887 metadata blocks”: 3 slices:
      • Other metadata: 1.8% (this step);
      • Inode tables: 97.0%;
      • Journal: 1.2%.
    • “Other metadata”, 7 labelled values:
      • Groups with copies: 25;
      • Blocks a copy: 1 + 2560;
      • All copies: 64,025;
      • Bitmaps: 327,680;
      • First files: 518;
      • Other metadata: 392,223;
      • Of the disk: 0.0073%.
  6. With -m 0, users can write 99.6%

    Only the kernel’s 4,096 blocks are held back; users gain 1 TiB.

    mkfs.ext4 -m 0 reserves nothing for root, so only the kernel’s 4,096 blocks are held back. Users can write 5,347,079,137 blocks, 268,435,456 more than at the default, 1 TiB. Root then gets no more room than other users.

    • Pie chart “Every 4 KiB block of 20 TiB”: 2 slices:
      • Users can write: 99.6% (changed);
      • Metadata: 0.4%.
    • “mkfs.ext4 -m 0”, 5 labelled values:
      • Root, 0%: 0;
      • Kernel: 4,096;
      • Users: 5,347,079,137;
      • Of the disk: 99.6%;
      • Gained: 1 TiB.
  7. At -m 0, users can write 93.5%, 98.3%, 99.6% of 1 GiB, 1 TiB, 20 TiB

    Metadata falls from 5.0% to 1.7% to 0.40% of the disk as disks grow.

    Metadata shrinks as a share of the disk, from 5.0% to 1.7% to 0.40%, because the journal stops at 1 GiB and larger disks get fewer inodes per byte. With no root reserve, almost everything else is for users.

    • “mkfs.ext4 -m 0, shares of each disk”, 3 labelled values:
      • 20 TiB: metadata 0.40%, root reserve 0.0%, users 99.6% (this scenario);
      • 1 GiB: metadata 5.0%, root reserve 0.0%, users 93.5%;
      • 1 TiB: metadata 1.7%, root reserve 0.0%, users 98.3%.

Check yourself

  1. On the 1 GiB disk the default reserve just covers the metadata. Going to 2 GiB doubles the reserve, and also doubles the journal, from 32 MiB to 64 MiB. Does the 5% reserve still cover all the metadata?

    Show the answer

    No, it falls just short: 26,214 reserved blocks against 26,284 of metadata, 0.997 times. The journal is most of the metadata on a disk this small, so doubling it grows the metadata about as fast as the 5% reserve. These are the counts a recorded mkfs.ext4 1.47.3 run at 2 GiB gives.

  2. At 4 TiB mke2fs switches to its big type, with one inode per 32 KiB of disk instead of 16 KiB. How does a 4 TiB disk’s metadata compare with that of a disk 128 MiB smaller?

    Show the answer

    The bigger disk has less. Its inode tables are about half the size, so its metadata is 8,750,621 blocks against 17,138,715, and users can write 32.1 GiB more although the disk grew by only 128 MiB.

  3. An administrator runs tune2fs -m 0 on the 1 TiB disk to reclaim the filesystem’s overhead. What do users get back, and what stays?

    Show the answer

    They get back the root reserve, 13,421,772 blocks or 51.2 GiB, which was free all along. The 4,495,258 blocks of inode tables, journal and other metadata stay, and so do the kernel’s 4,096.
