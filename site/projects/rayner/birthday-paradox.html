How many people make a shared birthday likely?

A year has 365 days, so a match can seem to need a crowd. Each card is one person. Before searching the dates, guess how likely a shared birthday is in a room this size. The chances on this page assume independent birthdays, and give every day of a 365-day year the same chance, with no 29 February.

  2. No shared birthday in this room of 10

    Is that luck, or usual for a room of 10?

    Most rooms of 10 have no match: of 1,000 random rooms, 112 had a shared birthday, and the exact chance is 11.7%. A room of 23 people gives a better than even chance that two share a birthday, because 23 people make 253 pairs, each a chance to match. The next steps count those pairs.

  6. 45 pairs at 1 in 365 each: about 11.6%

    Close to the exact 11.69%; overlapping comparisons need care.

    Treating the 45 pairs as independent gives the nearby shortcut shown. It is the chance you would have of sharing your own birthday with one of 45 other people. The room’s pairs are not jointly independent: if Ann matches Ben and Ben matches Cal, Ann must match Cal. The person-by-person calculation avoids that dependence. Comparisons with your one birthday do involve independent arrivals, so multiplying their miss chances gives the exact 2.4% for a match with you.

    • One pair shares a birthday: 1 in 365. All 45 pairs miss: 364/365, 45 times over. = 88.39% chance of no match. Pair shortcut. 11.61%. Exact. 11.69%. The gap: pairs that share a person.

Check yourself

  1. Two separate rooms each hold 23 people. When everyone joins one room, how many new pairs become possible, and why can’t you just add the two rooms’ match probabilities?

    Show the answer

    529 new pairs: each of the 23 people in one room can now match each of the 23 in the other. The combined room has 1,035 pairs, compared with 506 within the two separate rooms. Adding the old probabilities misses the cross-room pairs and counts overlapping match events twice.

  2. On a planet with 100 equally likely birthdays, would 23 people still be needed for a better than even chance of a shared birthday? Use the person-by-person calculation to find the smallest room.

    Show the answer

    No: 13 people suffice. Multiply the chances of avoiding earlier birthdays, 100/100 × 99/100 × … × 88/100, and subtract from 1: about 55.7%. With 12 people it is about 49.7%. A smaller calendar makes each new pair more likely to match.
